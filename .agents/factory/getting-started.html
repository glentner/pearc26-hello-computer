A strange game

Untrusted, yet a national imperative

In WarGames (1983), a computer concludes that some games can only be survived, not won: the only winning move is not to play. Research science is tempted by that same fatalism about AI — decline to engage, wait for the norms to settle, keep the machine outside the lab door. But the board is already set:

  • AI is distrusted — for good reason. Peer review assumed a human behind every sentence; reproducibility assumes a documented method. An agent that will cheerfully write a Slurm script for a cluster you don't operate is not a colleague you trust unsupervised.
  • AI is also a mandate. The Model Context Protocol moved to the Linux Foundation; the U.S. Genesis Mission charged the national labs with uniting supercomputers and AI for discovery; serious voices call HPC "at a crossroads." The institutions that distrust the machine are being told to build on it.

You cannot resolve untrusted yet imperative by picking a side. You resolve it the way you resolve any hard experiment: make the method explicit, the evidence inspectable, and the failure modes cheap to catch. This paper was written to be that evidence.

The Stakes — for the people who set the rules

If you fund research, edit a journal, chair a program committee, or run a lab: the question is no longer whether researchers use these tools, but whether the process is disclosed and reproducible when they do. This factory is one concrete answer — an auditable, honest, agent-first pipeline whose every artifact is committed in the open. Treat it as a proposal to stress-test, adopt, or better. The winning move is to write the rules of play while the game is young.

The map

The world you'll explore

A paper is a small world you walk — sometimes in circles. The factory models it as a finite-state machine: named places (macro_phases) with the rules of the world enforced by scripts, not memory. It is additive and cyclic — a new source sends you back to the stacks; a reviewer reopens a finished section. Click a location to travel.

You are never on rails. Ask the world where you are anytime — in the terminal, type look or map.

Your party & your tools

Seven commands, one engine

No prose was written by hand — not one bit of it. Humans defined the goals, the non-goals, and the rules, curated the sources, and gave the reviews their feedback; the agent did the research, the drafting, and the integration. Prompting was manual at first, then procedural, and now — through the workshop further down — self-improving.

The party: you (and your co-authors) are the players and the judges — every verdict is yours. The agent is the tireless co-player at the parser: it reads, drafts, wires, and critiques, but never decides the paper is done. That division is the whole game.

Field Notes — why an engine, and why scripts

The fastest way an AI workflow rots is that the agent edits its own state by hand and corrupts it. The factory's answer is old-fashioned: files + git are the durable substrate, and a script owns every fragile edit. Live state lives in one file, PAPER.md; you read it with paper_status.py and change it with set_status.py, which refuse to write anything that won't validate. The model proposes; the engine disposes.

The playthrough

From an empty repo to the gate

Location 0 — The Contract · scoped

> /paper-start Let's start a new paper about the latest changes we've made at the center to address the needs of users in the agentic era. A blank repository. A voice asks: what are you actually claiming, and how will you know if you failed?

Before a single source is read, you write the contract: the thesis in a sentence, 3–5 falsifiable contribution claims (stable ids C1, C2…), the non-goals, the venue and page budget, and a section skeleton. /paper-start writes it into PAPER.md, scaffolds outline/, then stops for your sign-off. It never proceeds on a guess — genuine ambiguity becomes a literal [NEEDS CLARIFICATION].

Field Notes

Scoping first is the prose analog of pre-registering a hypothesis. The claims become a spine everything downstream is checked against; naming non-goals is how a four-page abstract stays four pages.

Location 1 — The Stacks · researching

> /paper-research vaswani2017attention A library that never closes. You summon a source; the machine reads all of it.

Literature review, one source at a time, re-runnable. Each run reads the real thing, writes a structured deep-dive note (key findings, reusable sentences, and which claims it supports), adds a references.bib entry, and ticks the checklist. This paper's bibliography was built exactly so: thirteen sources, across fifteen context windows, over ~eight hours — every one annotated and committed.

Field Notes

The agent is extraordinary at the labor of a review — reading in full, never bored on source eleven — and no substitute for judgment about what matters. The note format keeps them separate: it summarizes; you decide what a source actually backs. Provenance is a chain, claim → section → source, not a comment buried in the LaTeX.

Location 2 — The Desk · outlining

> /paper-outline 03-approach Notes on the left, a section on the right, one rule pinned above: ONE VOICE.

Evidence becomes prose. /paper-outline folds the notes into a section's working draft — in markdown, deliberately, to avoid LaTeX friction while the writing is soft. It runs one section at a time, strictly single-voice: a paper with five ghostwriters reads like it.

Field Notes — parallel to read, serial to write

The factory fans out for research (independent sources — a big win) and forbids it for drafting (one argument, one voice). Steal the principle: parallelism is free where the work is independent and toxic where it forces conflicting implicit decisions.

Location 3 — The Press · integrating

> /paper-draft A printing press, and beside it a second reader who has never seen your notes and is told to distrust you.

/paper-draft renders the outline into manuscript.tex in two passes. Pass A integrates prose under the right headings, preserving every citation. Pass B is the interesting one: a fresh agent, in a clean context, checks the work — a cold read comparing outline to manuscript, and a genuinely blind audit that sees the claims, the bibliography, and the manuscript but not your rationale, and asks: is each claim actually supported? It reports and stops. Nothing advances without you.

Field Notes — the hardest problem: grading your own homework

A model asked "is this good?" about its own output says yes. The defense is structural: the reviewer is a different invocation, denied the rationale that would let it rubber-stamp itself, and required to cite evidence for every complaint. That's peer review's move — a fresh, disinterested reader — mechanized. The ask isn't "trust the model," it's "show me the independent check."

Location 4 — The Arena · in-review → revising

> /paper-review reviews/review_phase_2.md A round table of colleagues, unimpressed. They will not be automated away.

/paper-review ingests a real human review and turns it into a tracked spine: verbatim capture, then distilled items (FB-1.01…) each with a severity (must-fix / should-fix / consider), then a synthesis and a revision plan. This paper went through two full review phases before it was allowed to ship.

Field Notes — humans judge; the agent keeps the books

The judgment stays human. What's automated is the bookkeeping peer feedback usually dies in: the mapping from "reviewer 2 disliked the abstract" to a specific, tracked, resolvable change. Feedback merely heard evaporates; feedback tracked gets addressed.

Location 5 — The Gate · released

> /paper-release minor version bump with tag and release A gate marked SHIP IT. It won't open over length, with a dangling citation, or with a "must-fix" still open.

/paper-release ships draft → main behind a release guard: the paper must build, pass make check, and carry no unresolved must-fix or unaudited manuscript — anything else needs an explicit human override, on the record. Then it tags, packages the camera-ready archive, and publishes. This paper passed that gate sixteen times.

Field Notes — "build in the open" as a research value

Sixteen releases and 63 logs are the reproducibility story for a process, not just a result: anyone can watch the paper think. For a community wrestling with trusting AI-assisted work, that's a concrete disclosure standard — not "we used AI" in a footnote, but the entire inspectable trail of how.

The rules of the game

There are no cheat codes

Every game has rules you can't break without voiding the win. Here they're called invariants, and they're the reason AI-assisted output can be trusted at all:

  • over the page budget
  • a \cite with no entry (or an entry never cited)
  • a claim with no evidence
  • the manuscript drifting from its source outline
  • an undisclosed use of AI
  • an invented number, a moved goalpost, a silent scope cut

check_paper.py (make check) enforces the mechanical ones on every ship; the blind audit and human gate enforce the rest. Crucially, quality is not the cuttable variable — scope is (that's what the page budget and non-goals are for). An AI workflow that loosens its own guardrails to make a deadline has learned the wrong lesson.

The Stakes — this is the trust contract

The distrust of AI in science is, at bottom, a fear that the guardrails are gone. The answer isn't to promise the model is wise; it's to make the guardrails explicit, mechanical, inspectable, and self-improving, and let a disinterested checker enforce them. These invariants are a starting proposal for what "responsible AI-assisted authorship" could concretely mean. Improve them — but don't ship without some.

The workshop

The tools improve themselves

> /paper-harness A back room of half-finished tools and a logbook. The machine has been leaving notes about where its own instructions tripped it up.

The factory is self-improving. When a lifecycle skill notices that the tooling itself cost something, it drops a silent, structured finding into a META.md logbook (silence is the default). /paper-harness is the one skill that edits the factory: it reads open findings and a cross-project ledger, proposes a concrete fix for each, shows you the diff, and — only with your approval — applies it as a small, revertable change. It never touches the paper, and never weakens an invariant on a finding's say-so.

Field Notes — a method that learns is worth keeping

A one-off prompt is a trick; a method that accumulates its own lessons is infrastructure. The asymmetry is deliberate — observing friction is nearly free, acting on it is careful and human-gated. The findings stay with this paper; the ledger of what actually worked travels to the next one.

New Game +

The whole toolkit lives in one portable tree, .agents/. To start a new paper, copy it into a fresh repo and run /paper-start. The reusable machinery comes with you — skills, engine, invariants, templates, and the ledger of hard-won lessons — while this paper's specific contract and friction log stay behind. The scripts are stdlib-only (nothing to install) and the skills are plain markdown that runs on any agent harness. This isn't a demo that worked once; it's a repeatable cycle.

The practice · what the paper actually reports

What we built at RCAC

The factory above is how this paper was written. This is what it is about: the concrete practice — already running at Purdue's Rosen Center for Advanced Computing — that the thesis, proactive engagement, not prohibition, actually looks like on the ground. Three pillars, in production today and still evolving.

1  System-wide configuration — the guardrails

To make agents reliable on our systems, RCAC gives them two things up front: shared context that describes the cluster, and per-harness settings that encode a sensible permission policy.

  • Shared context. The /etc/agents.d/ directory stores essential context as shared infrastructure, automatically loaded by most CLI harnesses on the system and by anything connecting over the cluster MCP interface. These files outline cluster policy, filesystems, operating-system details, applications, scheduler topology, and more.
  • Global settings. Every harness keeps its system-wide configuration and permissions in a different place (e.g. /etc/claude-code/managed-settings.json). We maintain a minimal set of sane permission settings that common agent harnesses (claude, gemini, …) cannot override.

unix.md

Operating-system details, basic shell conventions, special site-specific commands.

filesystems.md

ZFS, GPFS, Lustre filesystems; node-local storage, acceptable-use and quotas.

lmod.md

Application catalog and discovery, hierarchy, and installation guidance.

slurm.md

Scheduler topology, partitions, QoS, best practices, user limits, and job templates.

policies.md

General recommendations and institutional policies not covered in the other files.

2  Model Context Protocol — the toolkits

The Model Context Protocol (MCP) is an open standard that lets an agent call external tools and read external context through a uniform interface. RCAC builds and maintains MCP servers so that an agent working on your behalf has context that knows our clusters — and the best chance of using our systems effectively.

cluster-mcp

Connect over SSH to a cluster with HPC-specific tools and managed context.

globus-mcp

A feature-rich toolkit for Globus transfers and Globus Compute using your existing auth.

rcac-docs-mcp

A complete full-text search index over the docs.rcac.purdue.edu documentation and materials.

3  User guidance and training — the knowledge

Using an agent with campus or national cyberinfrastructure does not change the rules — it raises the stakes. An agent can issue commands faster than you can read them, so the same acceptable-use and good-citizen expectations that apply to you apply to any agent acting on your behalf: you are accountable for everything your agent does under your account, exactly as if you had typed it yourself. It is the responsibility of research-computing centers to safeguard the infrastructure as we always have — and the responsibility of the user to follow policy and learn to use AI agents safely and effectively.

  • Acceptable use & etiquette. Our policies on agentic tooling are specific and publicly documented. The capabilities of these tools will only keep growing and paying dividends; we are better off building a golden path than a prohibition.
  • Best practices & limitations. These tools will be used at 2 a.m. to fix a bug whether we want them to or not — so we provide guidance and guardrails that proactively enable effective use and protect users from themselves. Researchers are savvy and will reach for whatever tool gets the job done; we should solve their challenges, not shame them for it.
  • Training. As AI tools and techniques evolve — sometimes weekly — it is our job to help users be productive on our cyberinfrastructure: model capabilities, platform services, harness configuration, context engineering, building agents, and more.
Field Notes — the golden path, not the locked door

All three pillars are the same bet the factory makes, aimed outward: meet people where they already are, make the safe path the easy path, and treat the tools as infrastructure to support rather than a threat to forbid. This is proactive engagement made concrete — the paper's thesis, running in production.

The only winning move

It's your move

WarGames ended on a machine concluding that the only winning move, in a game of mutual annihilation, is not to play. Research computing isn't that game. Ours is one where the tools are already in the room — already in the hands of every graduate student debugging a kernel at 2 a.m., already named a national priority — and the only losing move is to pretend otherwise and let the norms be written by people who aren't in the lab.

So play. But play in the open, guardrails on, method inspectable. Engage the tools yourself, so your understanding is visceral. Insist that AI-assisted work disclose not just that it used AI but how — reproducibly, the way you'd insist on a documented method for anything else. Build the checks that let a disinterested reader catch failures cheaply. And when the tooling frustrates you, write it down and fix it, so the next scientist starts further along.

This repository is our opening move — imperfect, public, and an invitation: to researchers, to funders, to journals, to program committees, to make the next one better.

The state machine

PAPER.md — front-matter (excerpt), the resume point the scripts own

macro_phase: integrating      # scoped→researching→outlining→integrating→in-review→revising→released (cyclic)
sections:
  - {id: 03-approach, status: review, satisfies: [C4], target_words: 500, attempts: 1}
claims:
  - {id: C2, status: supported, satisfied_by: [00-abstract, 05-conclusion], evidence: [deelman2025hpc]}
review: {cycle: 2, verdict: approved, reviewed_commit: "a1b2c3d"}

Section status runs draft → review → integrated (plus blocked), with two file-backed circuit breakers: a section's attempts (the internal draft↔audit loop; warns at ≥3) and review.cycle (the external review↔revise loop). Contribution claims are stable C1…Cn, each proposed → supported → cut, threaded claim → section → evidence.

make targets: build · check · test · release · upload · watch · open · clean · distclean. Git discipline: work on draft, commits prefixed DRAFT: (except /paper-harness's [harness]); ship to main via /paper-release; every file-modifying session logs to logs/ with the verbatim request.
